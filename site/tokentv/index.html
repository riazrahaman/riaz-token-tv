Prologue

The clock thinks it is a photo frame 🖼️

These clocks ship with vendor firmware, no source, and no warranty worth the name. Replacing it means opening the case and soldering. So TokenTV doesn't. It uses a feature the stock firmware already has: a photo album with an HTTP upload endpoint, and a theme that shows a photo full-screen.

🖥️
A computer that stays on

Reads and draws

Reads your usage through each provider's own login, then draws a 240×240 picture with Pillow.

📤
Plain HTTP

Uploads a photo

Posts the image to the clock's album and selects the photo theme. No firmware, no agent, no SDK.

🕰️
The clock

Displays a picture

It has no idea what it is showing. That is the whole point: nothing on the device can break.

Why this constraint is the design. Everything awkward in the project descends from one fact: the only way to change a single pixel is to upload a whole image and have the device store it in flash. That is why there is a write budget, why countdowns are rounded, and why identical frames are never re-sent.

The RAM wall. A full 240×240 16-bit frame buffer is 115,200 bytes. The ESP8266 has roughly 40–50 KB of usable heap. No firmware for this board — vendor or open-source — can buffer a whole frame, so JPEGs are decoded in small tiles and pushed straight at the panel. It is also why rendering on a real computer and shipping one finished image is the right division of labour.
Chapter 02

From an API call to a picture 🔀

Python 3.10+ with Pillow as the only runtime dependency. No database, no queue, no container, no cloud. Persistent state is a handful of JSON files and the last image uploaded.

↔ scroll the diagram sideways

One clock, one owner. A second publisher pointed at the same clock fights over the filename and over which saved backup is authoritative. So the watchlist runner drops its device address entirely and becomes a pure feeder, writing only a snapshot file. The usage runner reads that file and stays the single process that backs up, uploads and re-asserts the display. The conflict is removed by construction, not by coordination.
Unknown is never zero. A failed read keeps the previous values and marks the row stale rather than reporting 0%. A missing reset time renders as a dash. An identity mismatch blanks the numbers instead of showing another account's. A wrong number is worse than no number on a display you glance at.

Chapter 04

The part that was actually hard 💾

The clock keeps every frame in flash, and flash wears out. The obvious guard — skip the upload when the bytes are identical — turns out not to be enough, and the reason is worth stating because it is easy to get wrong.

The dedupe never fires when it matters. When a short usage window leads, the face shows a countdown in minutes. That countdown changes on every poll, so every frame differs and the byte comparison never matches. Measured at the default five-minute poll: twelve distinct frames an hour, every hour you are working.
  1. Byte dedupe — an identical frame is never rewritten.
  2. Countdown bucketing — the countdown is rounded up to whole buckets, so the face changes when the remaining time crosses an edge rather than every minute. A steady hour drops from twelve writes to four.
  3. A spaced write budget — uploads are spaced rather than given a quota. A quota lets a busy hour spend its whole allowance in the first twenty-five minutes and then freeze the display on a stale frame, which is the opposite of useful.
  4. A data-change override — the readings are fingerprinted separately from the countdown, so a percentage or price that actually moved bypasses the budget and goes out at once. A budget that defers real news is worse than no budget.

↔ scroll the diagram sideways

A watchdog, because once was not enough. The call that selects the photo theme used to run only when the output filename changed — that is, once. A clock that reboots, or that someone opens in its own web UI, silently reverts to its own theme while uploads keep succeeding into a photo nobody is looking at. It is now re-asserted periodically.
A quality ladder, because the limit is hard. The firmware rejects an oversized upload outright. A busy face can cross that limit on its worst frame and simply stop updating with no visible error, so the encoder steps quality down, then chroma, until the frame fits, and records what it chose.

Two flash regions, and only one of them forgives you

Disassembling the firmware settled a question that had been shaping the design. The photo album is LittleFS — copy-on-write and wear-levelled, so a rewritten file lands on fresh blocks. That moved the wear estimate from months to the order of a decade, and demoted the write budget from a safety mechanism to good hygiene.

The settings are a different story. Everything written through the settings endpoint — brightness, the night window, the accent colours — lands in the ESP8266's emulated EEPROM: one 4 KB sector, rewritten whole on every commit, no wear levelling, holding the Wi-Fi credentials beside the display settings. That asymmetry kills an idea that looks attractive from the outside: using the accent colours as a cheap status channel, since they need no image upload. They are not cheap. They are the most fragile flash on the device.

Chapter 07

What would come next 🔭

Proposed · not built

The limitation worth attacking is not wear. It is the contract: the only way to change a pixel is to store a whole image. A custom firmware could change the device from a picture frame you rewrite into a display you draw on — accepting a frame over HTTP, decoding it straight to the panel in tiles, and never touching the filesystem at all. The write budget would stop existing, and every existing face would keep working, because the rendering stays on the host.

It is a weekend's work and a £5 serial adapter, plus a full flash backup first, because the vendor image restores the firmware but not the photos. Whether that is worth opening the case is a separate question from whether it would work.
